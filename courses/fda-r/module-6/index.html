Session 6.1

Event study: the COVID-19 lockdown and NIFTY 50

An event study measures how a specific piece of news moved a price, whether the news was a war, a policy announcement, an earnings surprise or a pandemic. The idea is to compare what actually happened with what would have happened without the event.

The timeline

WindowPurposeHere
Estimation windowEstimate "normal" returns120 trading days before the event window
Event day (t = 0)The first trading day under the news25 March 2020, the first session of India's national lockdown
Event windowMeasure the reactiont = −10 to t = +10 (21 trading days)

The formulas

  • Expected return under the constant-mean model: E[R] is the average return over the estimation window. The market model (E[Rᵢ] = α + βRₘ) or Fama–French are the research-grade alternatives.
  • Abnormal return: ARₜ = Rₜ − E[R]
  • Cumulative abnormal return: CAR = Σ ARₜ
  • t-statistics: t = AR₀ / σ_est for the event day, and t = CAR / (σ_est × √L) for a window of L days. If |t| > 1.96, the effect is significant at 5%.

How to read it

Two numbers tell the story. On the event day NIFTY actually rose, with a large positive abnormal return. Markets had sold off for weeks, and the relief rally came as the lockdown and early stimulus took shape. How to read it

The r = 0 statistic is below its 5% critical value, so we can't reject "no cointegration". NIFTY and the S&P 500 don't share a stable long-run path, so a VECM is the wrong tool. Use a VAR on the differenced (stationary) series instead. Note that ca.jo() needs K ≥ 2 even if the selected lag is 1.

How to read it

S&P → NIFTY: F ≈ 45 with a p-value of essentially zero, so the US Granger-causes India. NIFTY → S&P: p ≈ 0.06, which isn't significant at 5%. Causality is unidirectional, from the US to India. The VAR coefficients agree: sp500.l1 in the NIFTY equation is large and highly significant.

Watch out

Part of this is the clock. The US market closes about 10 hours after India's on the same calendar date. So a row that pairs "today's" S&P close with "tomorrow's" NIFTY is really pairing consecutive sessions, and one lag of the S&P is effectively news that India hasn't traded yet. Here the lead–lag is real, but it isn't mysterious.

Session 6.3

The machine learning framework for finance

Machine learning means programming computers to optimise a criterion from data, so that they learn the patterns themselves instead of being given an equation. It sits at the cognitive end of the analytics ladder from Session 1.1.

Why it matters in finance now: data has exploded, storage is cheap, decisions need to be fast, and markets are noisy and non-linear, which linear models miss.

Two families

Training dataLabelled (we know the answer)Unlabelled
Continuous targetRegression (predict the return)n/a
Categorical targetClassification (predict Up or Down)n/a
GoalPredictCluster similar observations
Finance exampleNext-day direction, credit defaultGrouping stocks by behaviour, fraud anomalies

The models in Session 6.4

ModelHow it worksUse it when…
Linear regressionA straight-line equationYou need an interpretable baseline
Decision treeA sequence of if/then splitsYou want readable trading rules or threshold effects
Random forestHundreds of trees, then a majority voteThe data is noisy and you need robustness
Support vector machine (SVM)Finds the widest boundary between classes, curved by a kernelUp and Down aren't separated by a straight line
Neural networkLayers of weighted nodesThere's lots of data and hidden interactions
Watch out

The traps that matter most in finance

Overfitting: a model that memorises the past and fails tomorrow.

Structural breaks: relationships change, for example after COVID.

Look-ahead bias: using information you wouldn't have had at the time.

Data snooping: re-running until something "works". Choosing the right design matters more than choosing a fancy algorithm.

Session 6.4

Machine learning in R: predicting NIFTY's direction

Target: will NIFTY close Up or Down tomorrow? The regression version predicts the size of the return.

Features, all known at the close of day t and used to predict day t+1:

  • the last three days' returns
  • the S&P 500's return
  • the 5-day and 20-day moving averages relative to price
  • the 14-day RSI
  • the 10-day volatility
  • the change in volume

R
library(TTR)
nf <- read.csv(paste0(url, "NIFTY50.csv")); sp <- read.csv(paste0(url, "SP500.csv"))
d <- na.omit(merge(data.frame(Date = nf$Date, close = nf$Close, volume = nf$Volume),
                   data.frame(Date = sp$Date, sp_close = sp$Close), by = "Date"))
d <- subset(d, Date >= "2018-01-01" & volume > 0)

d$ret     <- c(NA, diff(log(d$close))) * 100
d$sp_ret  <- c(NA, diff(log(d$sp_close))) * 100
d$lag1    <- d$ret; d$lag2 <- c(NA, head(d$ret, -1)); d$lag3 <- c(NA, NA, head(d$ret, -2))

d$next_ret  <- c(d$ret[-1], NA)

Watch out

The lecture uses a random 80/20 split (sample()) and same-day features. On time series both leak the future into training and inflate accuracy. Here we train on 2018 to early 2024 and test only on the later period the model has never seen. That's how you'd use the model in practice.

5. Neural network

Neural nets need inputs on a common scale. Scale the test set with the training set's min and max, so no test information leaks back.

R
library(neuralnet)
mins <- sapply(train[predictors], min); maxs <- sapply(train[predictors], max)
sc <- function(df) as.data.frame(scale(df[predictors], center = mins, scale = maxs - mins))
nn_train <- sc(train); nn_train$Up <- as.numeric(train$Direction == "Up")
set.seed(123)
nn_model <- neuralnet(as.formula(paste("Up ~", paste(predictors, collapse = " + "))),
                      data = nn_train, hidden = 3, linear.output = FALSE, stepmax = 1e6)
nn_prob <- compute(nn_model, sc(test))$net.result
nn_pred <- factor(ifelse(nn_prob > 0.5, "Up", "Down"), levels = c("Down", "Up"))
confusionMatrix(nn_pred, test$Direction, positive = "Up")$overall[c("Accuracy", "AccuracyNull", "AccuracyPValue")]

How to read it

The dashed line is the No Information Rate (about 51%): the accuracy of always predicting the more common outcome. Honest out-of-sample models beat it by only 3–7 points. The simple decision tree does best (58%, significantly above the NIR), and almost all of its edge comes from one feature, the overnight S&P move. The fancier models don't win. The lecture's 60–73% figures come from same-day features and a random split, so this gap is the most valuable lesson in the module.

Watch out

Even 58% direction accuracy isn't money in the bank. By the time NIFTY opens, the S&P's overnight move is already in the opening price, so a close-to-close prediction doesn't translate directly into a trade. Measure ML trading ideas on returns after costs, not on accuracy.

Try it

Recreate the lecture's setup to see the inflation for yourself. Use same-day sp_ret as a feature with today's direction as the target, then split with sample(). Accuracy jumps, because the S&P's same-day close already contains much of NIFTY's next-morning news.

The idea

Accuracy or interpretability? A linear model or a single tree is easy to explain to a risk committee. A forest or a neural network may squeeze out a little more accuracy. In finance, a model nobody can explain rarely makes it to production.
