Session 7.1

Diagnosing poor outputs

When an output is poor, the temptation is to rephrase and try again. That sometimes works by luck. A better habit is to diagnose first: name the symptom, find the likely cause, and change the thing that's actually wrong. Almost every problem traces back to one of six areas covered in this course.

SymptomLikely causeWhere to fix itModule
Generic, could apply to anyoneMissing contextAdd audience, purpose, specifics1
Right content, wrong shapeNo format specifiedState length, structure, destination1
Inconsistent from run to runNo examples, vague instructionsAdd examples; tighten instructions1, 5
Wrong or invented factsNo source, or no "I don't know" optionGround in documents; allow uncertainty; verify2
Outdated informationOld knowledge file, or no web accessUpdate knowledge; use web search or Research3, 5
Shallow on a hard taskTask too big, or model too smallDecompose; try a more capable model1, 3
Forgets earlier decisionsLong, cluttered threadHandoff summary and fresh chat; persist in Project3
Ignores a Project ruleRule in the description, or conflicting instructionsMove it to instructions; remove conflicts5
The idea

Fix the cause, not the output. Editing a bad answer by hand fixes it once. Changing the prompt, the instructions or the knowledge fixes it for every future answer. When the same correction keeps coming up, it belongs upstream.

Finding the pattern in a week of problems

When a team uses Claude daily, individual complaints hide a pattern. Logging each poor output with a rough cause shows where to spend effort. This is a simple Pareto analysis: sort causes by frequency, and see how few causes account for most problems.

Python
from collections import Counter

log = [  # one entry per output a reviewer flagged this week (cause chosen from the table above)
    "missing context", "wrong format", "outdated info", "missing context", "invented fact",
    "missing context", "wrong format", "outdated info", "outdated info", "missing context",
    "too big a task", "outdated info", "missing context", "wrong format", "ignored project rule",
    "missing context", "outdated info", "wrong format", "missing context", "invented fact",
]
counts = Counter(log).most_common()
total, running = len(log), 0
for cause, n in counts:
    running += n
    print(f"{cause:<22} {n:>2} {'█' * n:<9} cumulative {running / total:5.0%}")
Output · produced by running this code
missing context         7 ███████   cumulative   35%
outdated info           5 █████     cumulative   60%
wrong format            4 ████      cumulative   80%
invented fact           2 ██        cumulative   90%
too big a task          1 █         cumulative   95%
ignored project rule    1 █         cumulative  100%
How to read it

Three causes account for 80% of this week's problems: missing context, outdated information and wrong format. That points to three concrete fixes: add audience and purpose to the team's prompt templates, replace the stale file in the Project knowledge, and add a format rule to the instructions. The rare causes (an invented fact, a too-big task) still matter, especially if they're high-stakes, but they aren't where most of the time is going.

Exam lens

Troubleshooting questions usually describe a symptom and offer several fixes. Pick the one that addresses the root cause the scenario points to (missing context, stale knowledge, wrong model or feature), not the one that just tries harder ("ask again", "use capital letters", "say please").

Practice questionA Project keeps quoting last quarter's prices even though the team told Claude the new prices in several chats. What is the most likely root cause and fix?

Context isn't shared across chats within a Project unless it's in the knowledge, and the old file is still the source. Fixing the knowledge fixes every future chat.

Practice questionOutputs for a recurring task are well written but vary in structure every time. Which change addresses the cause?

Inconsistent structure usually means Claude hasn't been shown the target structure. An example plus a clear format rule is the targeted fix.

Session 7.2

Adjusting from feedback

Feedback tells you whether a change worked. Sources include what users say, what reviewers correct, and how much each draft had to be edited before it was used. The exam objective is to adjust your approach based on feedback and results, which means acting on evidence, not on the last complaint you heard.

Feedback sourceWhat it tells you
How much drafts are edited before useWhether the output is close to what's needed
Reviewer corrections, by typeWhich instruction or source is causing errors
User ratings or commentsWhether people find it useful, and why not
Re-test results (Module 5)Whether a change fixed the problem without breaking others

Measuring how much drafts are edited

When people edit Claude's drafts before sending them, the size of the edit is free, objective feedback. Python's built-in difflib gives a similarity ratio between the draft and the final version (1.0 means unchanged). Low similarity on one template, week after week, says that template's prompt needs work.

Python
import difflib

pairs = {  # template: (Claude's draft, what staff actually sent)
    "booking confirmation": ("Your appointment at our Bandra clinic is confirmed for Tue 14 Mar at 11 am. Reply C to cancel.",
                             "Your appointment at our Bandra clinic is confirmed for Tue 14 Mar at 11 am. Reply C to cancel."),
    "reminder":             ("Reminder: your dental appointment is tomorrow at 4 pm at our Andheri clinic.",
                             "Reminder: your dental appointment is tomorrow at 4 pm at our Andheri clinic. Please bring your X-rays."),
    "post-treatment care":  ("Thank you for visiting us today. We hope your experience was pleasant and look forward to seeing you again.",
                             "After your filling today: avoid hot drinks for 2 hours and chew on the other side until tomorrow. Call us if pain lasts beyond 3 days."),
}
for name, (draft, final) in pairs.items():
    ratio = difflib.SequenceMatcher(None, draft, final).ratio()
    verdict = "fine" if ratio > 0.9 else "minor edits: consider a small prompt tweak" if ratio > 0.6 else "rewritten: fix the template's prompt"
    print(f"{name:<22} similarity {ratio:.2f} -> {verdict}")
Output · produced by running this code
booking confirmation   similarity 1.00 -> fine
reminder               similarity 0.85 -> minor edits: consider a small prompt tweak
post-treatment care    similarity 0.35 -> rewritten: fix the template's prompt
How to read it

Booking confirmations go out unchanged. Reminders get a small addition ("bring your X-rays"), worth adding to the template. The post-treatment message is essentially rewritten: Claude produced a generic thank-you, but staff needed specific aftercare instructions. That's a missing-context problem, and the fix is to give the template the treatment type and the clinic's approved aftercare notes, which are clinical content a dentist should have approved first.

Closing the loop

Watch out

Don't over-react to a single piece of feedback. One complaint can be a one-off; a pattern across many outputs is a signal. And when you change something, check that it didn't make other outputs worse: re-run the standard test requests (Module 5).

Practice questionStaff heavily rewrite one particular type of Claude draft every week, while other drafts are sent almost unchanged. What is the best response?

Consistent heavy edits on one draft type point to a specific gap in that template's prompt or context. Fixing it there improves that output without disturbing the ones that already work.

Session 7.3

Optimising workflows

Once outputs are good, the next step is making the whole workflow efficient and effective: less time, fewer steps, lower cost and fewer errors. Most of the levers have appeared earlier in the course.

LeverHow it helpsFrom
Reusable prompt templatesNo rewriting the same request each timeModule 1
Projects with instructions and knowledgeNo re-uploading files or re-explaining contextModule 5
The right model for each stepFaster, cheaper models for routine work; capable ones for hard stepsModule 3
BatchingOne request handles many similar itemsModule 3
Structured outputResults drop straight into spreadsheets or toolsModule 2
Fewer review rounds, risk-matchedSpot-check low-risk items; fully review high-risk onesModule 2
ConnectorsPull data from Drive, Gmail or Calendar instead of copy-pastingModule 5

Before and after

The workflow below is a monthly patient-feedback report, first as an ad hoc process and then optimised with the levers above. Time is in minutes per month.

Python
before = [  # step, minutes
    ("Export feedback from forms; copy into a chat",           25),
    ("Re-explain context and report format",                    15),
    ("Prompt, read, re-prompt (about 4 rounds)",                60),
    ("Reformat themes into the report template by hand",        45),
    ("Fact-check every number against the export",              40),
    ("Owner review and edits",                                  30),
]
after = [
    ("Feedback sheet pulled from Drive via the connector",      5),
    ("'Feedback report' Project: instructions + template",      0),
    ("One prompt from the saved template (1–2 rounds)",         15),
    ("Themes returned as a table, pasted into the template",    10),
    ("Spot-check: verify the top 5 figures against the sheet",  15),
    ("Owner review and edits",                                  20),
]
for name, steps in [("before", before), ("after", after)]:
    total = sum(m for _, m in steps)
    print(f"{name:<7} {total:4d} min/month ({total / 60:.1f} h)   steps: {len(steps)}")
saved = sum(m for _, m in before) - sum(m for _, m in after)
print(f"\nsaved {saved} min a month ({saved / sum(m for _, m in before):.0%}); "
      f"biggest single gain: prompting rounds {60 - 15} min")
Output · produced by running this code
before   215 min/month (3.6 h)   steps: 6
after     65 min/month (1.1 h)   steps: 6

saved 150 min a month (70%); biggest single gain: prompting rounds 45 min
How to read it

The monthly report drops from about 3.6 hours to just over an hour, a 70% saving. The largest gain comes from not re-prompting four times, thanks to a saved template inside a Project. Note what wasn't removed: fact-checking became a targeted spot-check of the figures that matter, and owner review stayed. Optimising means removing waste, not removing the safeguards from Module 2.

Tip

Stop optimising when the next gain isn't worth the effort. Shaving the last five minutes off a monthly task rarely matters; making a daily, high-volume task reliable usually does. Use the value thinking from Module 4.

Practice questionA team re-uploads the same three reference documents and re-explains the report format every week. Which optimisation helps most?

Repeated setup is the waste here, and Projects with saved instructions and knowledge remove it. A bigger model doesn't remove repeated work, and skipping review trades time for risk.

Practice questionSelect 2Which TWO changes optimise a workflow without weakening its safeguards? (Select 2)

Risk-matched review and model choice based on testing improve efficiency while keeping proportionate oversight. Removing review of external messages, or verification altogether, gives up the safeguards.

Session 7.4

Bringing it together for the exam

That completes the seven domains. Before you book the exam, run through this checklist against the official blueprint.

DomainWeightCan you…
1. Prompting and Task Execution14%Write a prompt with role, task, context, format and constraints; decompose a big task; iterate against criteria?
2. Output Evaluation and Validation21%Spot likely hallucinations, verify against sources, decide the level of human review, pick the output format?
3. Product and Model Selection12%Choose between chat, Projects, Research and artifacts, and between Haiku, Sonnet and Opus? Manage long contexts?
4. Workflow Integration and Solution Design16%Analyse a use case, map a process, augment or redesign it, and explain value and limits honestly?
5. Configuration and Knowledge Management12%Set up a Project with instructions and knowledge, manage connectors, maintain it over time?
6. Governance, Risk, and Responsible Use15%Recognise high-risk uses, protect sensitive data, follow policy, weigh ethics?
7. Troubleshooting and Optimization10%Diagnose root causes, act on feedback, optimise without weakening safeguards?
Exam lens

How the questions tend to work. Most are scenarios with one best answer, or a stated number of answers to select. The best answer is usually the one that is specific, evidence-based, proportionate to the risk, and within an Associate's scope (escalating technical work). Answers that rely on Claude's self-confidence, "trying harder", skipping review or ignoring policy are usually distractors.

Tip

Read the official Exam Guide once more before booking: check the current fee, the format, and the identification and retake rules. It's linked from this course's home page. Good luck.

Practice questionA scenario offers four fixes for an underperforming workflow. Which kind of answer is usually best on this exam?

The exam rewards targeted, evidence-based, proportionate action within the Associate role. The other options are common distractors.
