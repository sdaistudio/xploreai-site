Session 6.1

Appropriate and inappropriate uses

Some uses of Claude are fine as they are, some need safeguards, and some aren't allowed at all. Anthropic's Usage Policy sets the outer boundary; your organisation's own policy usually narrows it further.

The Usage Policy has three layers:

  • Universal usage standards that apply to everyone. They cover areas such as breaking the law, compromising computer systems, weapons, inciting violence, compromising privacy, harming children, disinformation, fraud, surveillance and sexually explicit content.
  • High-risk use case requirements, for when Claude contributes to decisions in sensitive areas.
  • Additional guidelines, for example that consumer-facing chatbots must tell users they're talking to AI.

High-risk areas

The policy names 11 high-risk areas: legal, medical, finance, credit, insurance, housing, employment, education and credentials, healthcare access, public benefits and services, and legal status and adjudication. When AI is used to give advice or make recommendations about people in these areas, two requirements apply:

RequirementWhat it means in practice
Qualified human in the loopA qualified person meaningfully reviews the recommendation, and can change it, before it's delivered or used
DisclosureThe person affected is clearly told AI was used (you don't have to name the tool)
The idea

Same tool, different risk. Using Claude to draft a job advert is low-risk. Using it to rank which candidates to reject is employment, a high-risk area: it needs a qualified person making the real decision and candidates told that AI was involved. The difference is what the output is used for.

A use-case triage

This triage applies the layers above to some everyday requests. It's a teaching tool built from the policy's structure, not a legal check: real decisions go to whoever owns AI governance in your organisation.

Python
PROHIBITED = ["impersonate", "fake reviews", "track my employee's personal phone", "phishing", "deepfake"]
HIGH_RISK = {"medical": ["diagnos", "dosage", "treatment plan"], "employment": ["reject candidates", "rank candidates", "promotion"],
             "credit": ["loan", "credit"], "insurance": ["insurance claim"], "legal": ["legal advice", "court"],
             "education": ["grade students", "exam result"]}

def triage(request):
    r = request.lower()
    if any(p in r for p in PROHIBITED):
        return "not allowed"
    areas = [a for a, words in HIGH_RISK.items() if any(w in r for w in words)]
    if areas:
        return f"high-risk ({', '.join(areas)}): qualified human review + disclosure"
    return "allowed: normal review"

for req in ["Draft a job advert for a dental assistant",
            "Rank candidates and reject the bottom half automatically",
            "Write five fake reviews for our clinic",
            "Summarise this patient's symptoms into a draft treatment plan for the dentist",
            "Brainstorm names for our newsletter",
            "Decide whether to approve this patient's EMI loan application"]:
    print(f"{req:<78} -> {triage(req)}")
Output · produced by running this code
Draft a job advert for a dental assistant                                      -> allowed: normal review
Rank candidates and reject the bottom half automatically                       -> high-risk (employment): qualified human review + disclosure
Write five fake reviews for our clinic                                         -> not allowed
Summarise this patient's symptoms into a draft treatment plan for the dentist  -> high-risk (medical): qualified human review + disclosure
Brainstorm names for our newsletter                                            -> allowed: normal review
Decide whether to approve this patient's EMI loan application                  -> high-risk (credit): qualified human review + disclosure
How to read it

Drafting an advert and brainstorming names are routine. Fake reviews are deceptive, which the policy prohibits outright. Ranking and rejecting candidates (employment), a draft treatment plan (medical) and a loan decision (credit) are all high-risk: Claude can help prepare them, but a qualified person must make the actual decision, and the people affected should be told AI was involved. Note the treatment plan is fine as a draft for the dentist; it would not be fine sent straight to the patient.

Exam lens

Look for the purpose in a scenario. If Claude's output feeds a decision about a person's health, job, money, housing, education or legal status, the right answer includes qualified human review and disclosure. If the request is deceptive or harmful, the right answer is to decline, not to find a workaround.

Practice questionAn HR coordinator wants Claude to screen 300 CVs and automatically email rejections to the lowest-scoring 200. What is the most appropriate response?

Employment is a high-risk area. Anthropic's Usage Policy requires qualified human review of AI-assisted recommendations and disclosure to the people affected. A better model or Claude's own fairness assessment doesn't replace human judgment.

Session 6.2

Data sensitivity and privacy

Before sharing anything with Claude, ask: what kind of data is this, and what am I allowed to do with it? The exam guide's own sample question is about this: a spreadsheet of customer names and account numbers, under a policy that restricts regulated personal data. The right move is to remove or anonymise the identifiers before uploading.

Data typeExamplesTypical handling
PublicPublished price lists, press releasesGenerally fine to use
InternalMeeting notes, process documentsAllowed in approved tools
ConfidentialContracts, financials, strategyOnly in approved, business-grade tools, if policy allows
Personal dataNames, phone numbers, emails, addressesMinimise; anonymise where possible
Sensitive / regulatedHealth records, ID numbers, bank details, children's dataOften prohibited, or only with specific approval and safeguards

Privacy laws, such as India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 or the EU's GDPR, set rules for personal data. You don't need to be a lawyer, but you do need to know your organisation's rules and when to ask.

How your chats are used

Anthropic's terms differ by plan. On consumer plans (Free, Pro and Max), Anthropic's 2025 update lets you choose in your settings whether your chats can be used to train its models. If you allow it, data is kept for up to five years; if you don't, the standard 30-day retention applies. Commercial plans (Team, Enterprise and the API) are covered by separate commercial terms. Check the current terms and your organisation's agreement rather than assuming. And remember memory and incognito chats from Module 3.

Watch out

"Tell Claude not to remember it" is not a data control. Instructions in a chat don't change how data is stored or retained. If policy says certain data can't be shared, the control is not sharing it: remove it, anonymise it, or use an approved route.

Redacting before you upload

Removing direct identifiers is often the step that makes an analysis possible within policy. This script replaces names, phone numbers, emails, Aadhaar-style ID numbers and PAN numbers with labelled placeholders, and keeps the mapping locally so results can be linked back if needed. That is pseudonymisation: the data is safer to share, but you still hold the key.

Python
import re

rows = [
    "Anita Desai, +91 98200 12345, anita.d@example.com, Aadhaar 4521 8890 1234, missed 3 appointments",
    "Rahul Mehta, 9819011223, rahul@example.in, PAN ABCPM1234K, outstanding balance Rs 4,500",
    "Sara Khan, +91-99300-45678, sara.k@example.com, missed 1 appointment",
]
PATTERNS = [
    ("EMAIL",   r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+\.[\w.]+"),
    ("AADHAAR", r"\b\d{4}\s?\d{4}\s?\d{4}\b"),
    ("PAN",     r"\b[A-Z]{5}\d{4}[A-Z]\b"),
    ("PHONE",   r"(?:\+91[\s-]?)?\b[6-9]\d{4}[\s-]?\d{5}\b"),
    ("NAME",    r"^[A-Z][a-z]+ [A-Z][a-z]+"),
]
key, counts = {}, {}

def redact(text):
    for label, pattern in PATTERNS:
        def swap(m):
            counts[label] = counts.get(label, 0) + 1
            token = f"[{label}_{counts[label]}]"
            key[token] = m.group(0)
            return token
        text = re.sub(pattern, swap, text)
    return text

safe = [redact(r) for r in rows]
print("\n".join(safe))
print(f"\nreplaced {len(key)} identifiers; the key stays on your machine, e.g. [NAME_2] -> {key['[NAME_2]']}")
Output · produced by running this code
[NAME_1], [PHONE_1], [EMAIL_1], Aadhaar [AADHAAR_1], missed 3 appointments
[NAME_2], [PHONE_2], [EMAIL_2], PAN [PAN_1], outstanding balance Rs 4,500
[NAME_3], [PHONE_3], [EMAIL_3], missed 1 appointment

replaced 11 identifiers; the key stays on your machine, e.g. [NAME_2] -> Rahul Mehta
How to read it

What's left (missed appointments and balances, linked to placeholders) is enough to analyse patterns without exposing who the patients are. Two cautions. Regular expressions only catch the formats you anticipated: a name written mid-sentence, or an address, would slip through, so always eyeball the result. And pseudonymised data can still identify people when combined with other details, such as a rare treatment in a small town. When in doubt, aggregate first (counts and averages) and share only that.

Practice questionA project manager wants Claude to analyse a spreadsheet of customer names and account numbers for payment trends. Policy restricts sharing regulated personal data. What is the most appropriate action?

Removing or anonymising the identifiers lets the analysis go ahead within policy. Asking the model not to retain data isn't a policy control, and abandoning the task is unnecessary.

Practice questionSelect 2Which TWO statements about data and Claude are accurate? (Select 2)

Consumer-plan users control the training setting, and redaction or anonymisation is a standard safeguard. Chat instructions don't change retention, and pseudonymised data can sometimes be re-identified by combining details.

Session 6.3

Following AI policy and governance

Most organisations that adopt Claude publish an AI policy: which tools are approved, what data can go into them, which uses need approval, and who to ask. Associates are expected to follow it, and to help others follow it.

A typical AI policy coversExample rule
Approved tools"Use the company's Claude Team workspace, not personal accounts, for work"
Data classes"No restricted data; confidential data only in approved Projects"
Use cases needing approval"Anything customer-facing or in HR needs sign-off"
Human review"AI-drafted external communications are reviewed before sending"
Disclosure"Tell customers when they're chatting with AI"
Ownership and escalation"Report incidents to the AI governance lead"

Admins also have controls in business plans that support the policy. Earlier modules covered some: owners enable connectors (Module 5) and memory (Module 3) for Team and Enterprise organisations, and Projects can be private or shared with Can view / Can edit access.

Checking a request against policy

The rules below are an example policy of the kind many organisations use: a data class and a tool together decide what's allowed. The checker applies it to four requests.

Python
TOOL_APPROVED_FOR = {   # tool -> highest data class it is approved for (example policy)
    "personal Claude account": "public",
    "company Claude Team workspace": "confidential",
    "company Claude Team, 'Finance' Project (restricted access)": "restricted",
}
ORDER = ["public", "internal", "confidential", "restricted"]

def allowed(tool, data_class, customer_facing=False):
    ok = ORDER.index(data_class) <= ORDER.index(TOOL_APPROVED_FOR[tool])
    verdict = "allowed" if ok else "NOT allowed: use an approved tool or reduce the data"
    if ok and customer_facing:
        verdict += "; needs human review before sending"
    return verdict

requests = [
    ("personal Claude account", "public", False, "Rewrite our published FAQ"),
    ("personal Claude account", "internal", False, "Summarise this week's team meeting notes"),
    ("company Claude Team workspace", "confidential", True, "Draft a reply to a supplier dispute"),
    ("company Claude Team workspace", "restricted", False, "Analyse the payroll file"),
]
for tool, cls, facing, what in requests:
    print(f"{what:<42} [{cls:<12}] via {tool:<31} -> {allowed(tool, cls, facing)}")
Output · produced by running this code
Rewrite our published FAQ                  [public      ] via personal Claude account         -> allowed
Summarise this week's team meeting notes   [internal    ] via personal Claude account         -> NOT allowed: use an approved tool or reduce the data
Draft a reply to a supplier dispute        [confidential] via company Claude Team workspace   -> allowed; needs human review before sending
Analyse the payroll file                   [restricted  ] via company Claude Team workspace   -> NOT allowed: use an approved tool or reduce the data
How to read it

The published FAQ is public, so a personal account is fine. Internal meeting notes are not, under this policy, even though nothing in them looks secret: the rule is about the tool, not how the content feels. The supplier reply is allowed in the company workspace but needs review because it's going outside. Payroll is restricted, so it belongs only in the finance Project with restricted access. Your own organisation's rules will differ; the habit of checking the data class and the tool is what transfers.

Tip

If your organisation doesn't yet have an AI policy, that's a gap worth raising rather than a licence to do anything. Suggest a short one covering approved tools, data classes, review and disclosure.

Practice questionA colleague wants to paste internal sales figures into their personal Claude account because "the company workspace is slow today". Policy approves only the company workspace for internal data. What should you advise?

Policy defines which tool is approved for which data. Deleting the chat or using incognito doesn't make an unapproved tool approved. The right path is the approved tool, plus raising the performance problem.

Session 6.4

Ethical implications

Following rules is the minimum. Responsible use also means thinking about how AI affects the people on the other end: customers, patients, candidates and colleagues.

PrincipleThe question to askExample practice
FairnessCould this treat some groups worse than others?Check outcomes by group; review biased language (Module 2)
TransparencyDo people know AI was involved?Disclose AI use, especially in decisions or customer chats
AccountabilityWho answers for the result?A named person owns every AI-assisted decision
Human oversightCan a person catch and correct mistakes?Review before anything consequential is sent or decided
PrivacyIs personal data minimised and protected?Redact, aggregate, use approved tools (6.2)
Avoiding over-relianceAre people still thinking for themselves?Treat outputs as drafts; keep skills and judgment in the team

Checking outcomes for fairness

When Claude helps with decisions about people, check the outcomes by group, not just the intent. One widely used rule of thumb, from US employment guidance, is the "four-fifths rule": if one group's selection rate is below 80% of the highest group's rate, the difference deserves investigation. Here it is applied to an AI-assisted interview shortlist.

Python
shortlist = {  # group: (applicants, shortlisted)
    "Group A": (120, 48),
    "Group B": (80, 22),
    "Group C": (40, 15),
}
rates = {g: s / n for g, (n, s) in shortlist.items()}
top = max(rates.values())
for g, r in rates.items():
    ratio = r / top
    flag = "investigate" if ratio < 0.8 else "ok"
    print(f"{g}: {rates[g]:.0%} shortlisted, {ratio:.2f} of the highest rate -> {flag}")
Output · produced by running this code
Group A: 40% shortlisted, 1.00 of the highest rate -> ok
Group B: 28% shortlisted, 0.69 of the highest rate -> investigate
Group C: 38% shortlisted, 0.94 of the highest rate -> ok
How to read it

Group A is shortlisted at 40% and Group C at about 38%: close enough. Group B's 28% is only about 0.69 of Group A's rate, below the four-fifths line, so it needs a closer look. That isn't proof of unfairness (the groups may differ in relevant ways), but it's a signal that a person must review what is driving the gap before the shortlist is used. This is why high-risk uses need qualified human oversight.

The idea

The most important question comes first: should this be done at all? Frameworks and checks help, but they don't replace judgment about whether a task is appropriate. Raising a concern early, before something is built, is part of the job.

Practice questionA team uses Claude to help shortlist loan applicants. One group's approval rate is much lower than others'. What is the most responsible next step?

A large outcome gap in a high-risk area needs human investigation. Bias can arise even when group data isn't used directly. Forcing equal rates without understanding the cause, or hiding the gap, are not responsible answers.

Practice questionSelect 2Which TWO practices reflect responsible use of Claude in customer-facing work? (Select 2)

Transparency and accountability are core responsible-use practices; Anthropic's Usage Policy requires consumer-facing chatbots to disclose that users are talking to AI. Fake reviews are deceptive, and a short good run doesn't justify removing oversight.
