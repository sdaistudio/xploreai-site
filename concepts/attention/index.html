Eight pages that rebuilt how machines read

The paper is called Attention Is All You Need. It introduced the Transformer — the design inside ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini and nearly every large language model since. No maths background needed here: one sentence runs through the whole page, and you can poke at every step.

The problem

Before 2017, machines read one word at a time

The best translators were recurrent networks. They worked like a person reading through a letterbox: one word in view, a scribbled summary of everything so far, and no way to look back.

Recurrent models: one pass, left to right, everything squeezed through a single memory.

Two things went wrong

It was slow by design. Word 50 could not be processed until words 1–49 were done. GPUs are built to do thousands of things at once, and this architecture forbade it.

Distance hurt. To connect the first word to the last, information had to survive every step in between. The paper measures this as path length: in a recurrent model it grows with the sentence. Long-range links — a pronoun and the noun it refers to, a verb and its object ten words later — were exactly what kept breaking.

Attention already existed in 2017, but as a helper bolted onto a recurrent network. This paper's move was to throw the recurrence away and keep only the helper. Hence the title.

The one idea

Let every word ask every other word: how much do you matter to me?

Take the sentence The animal didn't cross the street because it was too tired. What does it mean? You knew instantly, and not from word order — you knew because tired is something an animal can be and a street cannot.

Attention is that move, written as arithmetic. Every word looks at all the others at once and comes back with a set of weights: 40% of my meaning comes from animal, 15% from tired, barely anything from the. Then it rewrites itself as that blend.

Because every word does this independently, they can all be done at the same time — thousands in parallel on a GPU. And any two words are one hop apart, whether they are neighbours or forty words apart. Both problems from the previous section vanish together.

Slow and forgetful, replaced by parallel and global — with no new maths, just a weighted average taken seriously.

Step one

Words become lists of numbers

An embedding. The paper uses 512 numbers per word; four readable ones are shown here. Nobody writes these by hand — training sets them.

Why numbers at all

A network can only multiply and add. So each word is given a list of numbers — a vector — and words used in similar ways end up with similar lists. Dog lands near animal; street lands somewhere else entirely.

In the real model each list holds 512 numbers and no single one means "living" or "place". The directions are a mix that training discovered. The toy axes above are just so that the next few sections show you something readable.

dmodel = 512 — the width every part of the Transformer agrees on, so pieces can be added together and stacked.

Step two

Nothing in attention knows word order. So order gets added in.

A weighted average does not care about sequence. To attention, dog bites man and man bites dog are the same bag of three vectors — and those two sentences matter very differently if you are the man.

The fix is almost blunt: before any attention happens, add a second vector to each word that encodes where it sits. The paper builds it from sine and cosine waves at many different frequencies — fast waves that flip every couple of words, slow waves that drift across the whole paragraph. Read together, they are a clock that stamps each position uniquely.

Why waves instead of just the numbers 1, 2, 3? Because the gap between two positions then becomes a fixed rotation of the wave. "Three words back" looks the same at position 5 and position 500, so the model can learn relative position once and apply it anywhere — including in sentences longer than any it was trained on.

The paper also tried letting the model learn position vectors from scratch (Table 3, row E). Scores were almost identical. The waves were kept for the extrapolation.

Drawn live from the paper's formula: sin and cos of pos / 10000^(2i/512). Down = position 1 to 48 in the sentence. Across = 64 of the 512 channels, fast waves on the left, slow on the right. Every row is a different barcode; that barcode is "where I am".

The mechanism

Query, Key, Value — the whole of attention in three words

This is the part worth slowing down for. Everything else in the Transformer is plumbing around it.

Every word writes all three from its own vector: a query for what it wants, a key advertising what it offers, a value holding what it passes on.

How one word updates itself

Think of a library. You hand in a query — what you are looking for. Each shelf has a key — a label saying what it holds. You compare your card against every label, and the better the match, the more of that shelf's value you take with you.

In the model, the comparison is a dot product: multiply the two lists number by number and add up. Aligned vectors give a big number, unrelated ones give a small one. Those numbers become percentages via softmax, and the word's new vector is that percentage blend of all the values.

A key point people miss: the same word produces a different query, key and value, through three separate learned matrices. What I'm looking for, what I advertise and what I hand over are three different things.

The formula

The one equation, read left to right

Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q·KT / √dk ) V

Equation 1. That is the paper's central claim in full.

Q·KT — every query meets every key. For a ten-word sentence that is a 10×10 grid of match scores, computed in one matrix multiplication, which is the operation GPUs are fastest at. This is where the speed comes from.

÷ √dk — the fix that makes it trainable. Add up 64 products and the totals get large; feed large numbers to softmax and it collapses to "this one word, nothing else", which stops learning. Dividing by the square root of the width keeps the spread sane. The paper devotes a footnote to it; the slider shows why.

softmax — turns the scores into percentages that add to 100.

… V — the weighted blend of values. That blend is the word's new meaning, in context.

Same five raw scores on both sides. Drag towards 512 and the unscaled softmax on the left gives one word everything — the gradient dies with it. The paper's divisor on the right holds steady.

Multi-head attention

Eight of these run side by side, each minding its own business

One round of attention produces one weighted average, and an average smooths things out. But a word needs several relationships at once: a verb needs its subject and its object; a pronoun needs its referent and the clause it sits in.

So the paper runs h = 8 attention mechanisms in parallel, each with its own projections, each looking at a narrower 64-number slice. Their outputs are glued together and mixed back to 512. Eight slices of 64 is 512, so eight heads cost about the same as one wide one — the parallelism is free.

Nobody assigns the jobs. Train the thing, then inspect the heads, and the division of labour is there: the paper's appendix shows one head tracking long-range verb dependencies and another resolving what its refers to. The playground above is five hand-made heads standing in for that.

Table 3, row A: one head scores 0.9 BLEU worse than eight. Thirty-two heads are worse again. Eight was the sweet spot.

The block

Attention is one layer of a block, and the block is stacked six times

Residual connection plus layer norm around each sub-layer. Figure 1, simplified.

Three plain pieces

The feed-forward network. After the words have mixed, each one is pushed through the same small network on its own — widened to 2048 numbers, trimmed back to 512. Attention gathers; this digests.

The residual connection. The input of each sub-layer is added back to its output. So a layer only has to learn what to change, not reproduce everything that was already fine. It also gives gradients a short road home, which is what makes a deep stack trainable at all.

Layer normalisation. Rescales the numbers after each addition so nothing drifts to extremes. Unglamorous and load-bearing.

Stack six of these. Early blocks pick up grammar and neighbours; later blocks work on meaning that needs the whole sentence — which is how it finally gets tied to animal: one layer notices tired belongs to living things, a later one uses that to settle the pronoun.

Encoder and decoder

Reading happens all at once. Writing happens one word at a time.

The paper was a translation paper, so the Transformer has two halves: an encoder that reads the source sentence, and a decoder that writes the translation while peeking back at the encoder.

Shading shows how much of this step's attention went to each English word.

The mask, row by row

Each row is one output word; each column is a word it may look at. The blank half is the mask — scores there are set to minus infinity before softmax, so the weight comes out as zero.

Why the mask exists

During training the model is shown the whole correct translation at once, so that every position can be learned in parallel. But word three must not be allowed to read word four, or the task becomes copying rather than predicting.

So the decoder's self-attention is masked: a position may attend to itself and everything before it, nothing after. Exactly the same triangle is what makes ChatGPT generate left to right, one token at a time, today.

The alignment shading here is illustrative, not measured. The order of the Hindi output is real though — मैं एक किताब पढ़ रहा हूँ puts the verb last, so the decoder has to walk the English sentence out of order. A left-to-right reader with one memory slot struggles with that; attention over the whole source does not.

The results

Better translations, for a fraction of the compute

What the numbers were

Quality. 28.4 BLEU English→German, more than 2 points above the previous best, ensembles included. 41.8 English→French, a single-model record.

Cost. The base model trained in 12 hours on eight P100 GPUs. The big one took 3.5 days. Competing systems used an order of magnitude more compute — the paper's own estimate is 3.3×1018 operations for the base model against 2.3×1019 for GNMT.

It generalised. With almost no tuning, the same architecture was pointed at English sentence parsing — a different task with a different output shape — and beat all but one previous model, even trained on just 40,000 sentences.

That last result is the one that mattered. It said the Transformer was not a translation trick. It was a general way to process a sequence.

Eight years on

What changed after, and what did not

The encoder half went one way. Keep only the reading side, train it to fill in blanked-out words, and you get BERT — the model that sat behind search ranking and classification for years.

The decoder half went the other. Keep only the writing side with its mask, train it to predict the next token on a very large amount of text, and you get the GPT line, Claude, Gemini, Llama. Chat is that loop: predict the next token, append it, predict again.

The block barely moved. Attention, feed-forward, residual, normalise — the stack you scrolled through is still the stack. What changed is scale (six layers became a hundred or more; 65 million parameters became hundreds of billions), the data, and engineering on the edges: faster attention kernels, better position schemes, mixtures of experts, cheaper caches for long contexts.

So when a model today loses the thread of a long document, or when a context window gets more expensive as it grows, you are meeting this paper's trade-off directly: every token attending to every token is n² work. That is the price paid for the constant path length that made it all work.

The architecture outlived the task it was designed for.

Glossary

The jargon, in one line each

Token

A word or word-piece — the unit the model actually handles. The paper used about 37,000 of them for German.

Embedding

The list of 512 numbers standing in for a token's meaning.

Self-attention

Attention inside one sentence: the words rewrite each other. Queries, keys and values all come from the same place.

Query · Key · Value

What I'm looking for · what I advertise · what I hand over. Three different projections of the same word.

Softmax

Turns a row of raw scores into percentages adding to 100.

dk = 64

The width of each head's query and key vectors. Its square root is the divisor in the formula.

Head

One independent attention mechanism. Eight run in parallel, each on a 64-wide slice.

Mask

The rule that stops a position reading anything to its right. It is why text is generated one token at a time.

Residual connection

Adding a sub-layer's input back onto its output, so deep stacks stay trainable.

BLEU

A translation score, 0–100, measuring overlap with human reference translations.
